Verdict: Needs surgery — you asked your biggest accounts if they'd like to pay less, and they said yes.

Trying to do: Decide to replace per-seat pricing with usage-based pricing, using top-10-customer preference as the justification.

Keep: Going to customers directly before touching pricing is the right instinct. Most companies skip that step entirely.

Change:
1. The sample is the population most likely to benefit from the switch. Large accounts often have seats sitting idle relative to usage; for them, usage-based is simply cheaper. "Our biggest customers want it" and "our biggest customers pay less under it" can be the same fact wearing different clothes, and the pitch doesn't separate them.
2. Stated preference isn't modeled revenue. Nothing here runs these 10 accounts' actual usage against a proposed usage-based rate card to show whether their revenue goes up, down, or flat. A preference with no dollar figure attached is a vibe, not a business case.
3. Ten accounts don't stand in for the base. The pitch generalizes a full pricing-model migration from a subset with no stated size relative to total customers or total revenue — nothing about how mid-market or long-tail accounts, who may have opposite incentives (predictability over optimization), would fare.

Risks: Usage-based pricing can flip the incentive from "grow seats" to "minimize usage," which could shrink expansion revenue even if it thrills the accounts optimizing their bill. Modeling historical usage against proposed rates for a broader account sample would settle it.
