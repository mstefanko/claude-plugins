Verdict: Worth a cheap test — the idea's fine, the pitch is running on vibes instead of a mechanism.
Trying to do: Turn "what's in my fridge" into a week of dinners via AI, for parents who are too busy to plan.
Keep: Meal planning for busy parents is a real, already-proven pain point — an entire market of meal-planning apps exists because of it, so the problem space needs no further validation.
Change:
1. The one thing the pitch calls the differentiator — knowing what's in the fridge — has no stated mechanism. If it's manual entry, a busy parent typing in every ingredient contradicts the "for busy parents" premise before the AI even runs. If it's photo recognition, that's an unproven, hard vision problem (mixed containers, half-used sauces, produce that isn't labeled) the pitch never claims to have solved. The core input step the whole feature depends on is missing.
2. "Every parent I have talked to says they would use it" is stated intent, not usage. It's a convenience sample of people the author personally spoke to, answering a hypothetical about a free, frictionless tool that doesn't exist yet — the kind of answer that's cheap to give and reliably overstates real adoption. Nothing here is evidence of demand beyond politeness.
Risks: Meal-planning-from-fridge apps already exist with AI backends; whether this needs a real edge over them to get parents to switch is unconfirmed. A 15-minute competitor scan would settle it.
Prove me wrong: Ship a manual-entry-only version (no vision AI) to the parents who said yes, and check whether they're still entering fridge contents and using the plan in week three.
Confidence: high — pitch is a single paragraph, no files or claims to check against code. Zero tool calls.
